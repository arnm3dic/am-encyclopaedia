## Summary

The owl carries two meanings that sit badly together: learning and death. Both
come from the same source — a bird that sees in the dark — read once as insight
and once as the gaze of something that belongs to the night.

## Core meanings

- **Wisdom** — nocturnal vision read as perception beyond the ordinary; reinforced in the West by the owl's attachment to Athena.
- **Death and ill omen** — a cry heard at night, near houses, by a creature rarely seen; across Mediterranean and many other traditions the owl announces rather than observes.
- **Night and the hidden** — the owl stands in for what is active while others sleep, including thought that goes on alone.

## By tradition

### Greek

The little owl (*glaux*) is Athena's bird and appears beside her on Athenian
coinage, the silver tetradrachm being the most widely circulated image of the
pairing. The association is with the goddess's domain — practical intelligence,
skilled judgment — rather than with book-learning, which is a later overlay.

### Roman

Largely inverted. The *strix* is a bird of ill omen, its call near a dwelling
taken as a portent of death, and Pliny records the alarm an owl in the city
could cause. The same bird that signalled divine favour in Athens signalled
disaster in Rome.

### Japanese

*Fukurō* is read as a charm through wordplay: the syllables support both 不苦労
("no hardship") and 福郎 ("fortune"), and owl figures are sold as luck tokens on
that basis. This is a pun-based meaning, not a continuation of the
wisdom-or-death pair, and worth keeping distinct from it.

## Inversions and tensions

Greek favour and Roman dread attach to the same bird in neighbouring cultures
within a few centuries — the clearest case in the collection of a symbol whose
meaning depends almost entirely on who is listening. The modern "wise old owl"
of children's books descends from the Greek side only, with the omen tradition
dropped; the omen reading survives instead in horror conventions.
